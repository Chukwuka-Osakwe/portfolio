## The ask

The team came to me with a request: *make the pay button bigger*. Conversion
was down and the button felt like the obvious lever. The request arrived with a
mockup attached — the same button, scaled up forty percent, in a slightly more
saturated green. Everyone had already agreed it was the fix; they just needed
someone to draw it. I pushed to understand the drop-off before touching the
button.

That pause wasn't popular. When a number is falling, motion feels like progress,
and "let me look first" can read as "let me delay." So I time-boxed it: two days
to either confirm the button hypothesis or replace it with a better one.

## What I found

Session replays told a different story. People weren't missing the button —
they were abandoning at the **address step**, two screens earlier, when an
unexpected shipping cost appeared. The button at the end was fine. Most users
never reached it.

I watched roughly sixty replays. A pattern repeated: a confident scroll through
the cart, a pause at shipping, a visible double-take at the new total, and then
a close. People weren't confused about *how* to pay — they'd decided not to.

### The numbers behind the replays

- **61%** of abandonment happened on the address/shipping step, not the payment step.
- The median session that abandoned had already spent over two minutes in the flow.
- Sessions that saw a shipping estimate earlier (logged-in users with a default
  address) abandoned far less.

That last point was the tell: the people who *weren't* surprised by shipping
mostly finished. The problem wasn't persuasion. It was timing.

## The decision

I argued for showing shipping costs up front rather than enlarging a control on
the final screen. This was less flashy and harder to ship, but it addressed the
actual cause.

1. Surface a shipping estimate on the cart screen, before the form.
2. Collapse the three-step form into one scrollable page.
3. Keep the pay button exactly as it was.

The third point mattered as much as the first two. Leaving the button untouched
was a way of saying, out loud, that the original hypothesis had been tested and
set aside — not ignored.

### Pushback I had to work through

- *"Showing shipping early will scare people off."* It surfaced the same number
  earlier, when they were less invested — easier to absorb than at the final step.
- *"One long page feels heavier than three small steps."* Perceived progress
  improved because people could see the whole shape of what they were committing to.
- *"We already built the bigger button."* We shipped it too, behind the real fix,
  so the comparison was honest.

## How it shipped

We rolled it out as an A/B test against the existing flow (and against the
bigger-button variant, so all three were measured side by side). The
shipping-estimate-on-cart change went out first; the single-page form followed
two weeks later once we were confident the estimate wasn't itself causing drop.

## Outcome

Checkout completion rose meaningfully, the bigger-button variant did effectively
nothing, and the "bigger button" debate never came back. More durably, the team
started asking "what did we *observe*?" before bringing me solutions to draw.

The lesson I keep returning to: **the stated problem is rarely the real one.**
